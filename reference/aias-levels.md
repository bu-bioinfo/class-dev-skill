# AI Assessment Scale (AIAS) — Reference

Primary source: Perkins, M., Roe, J., & Furze, L. (2025). "Reimagining the Artificial Intelligence Assessment Scale (AIAS): A refined framework for educational assessment." *Journal of University Teaching and Learning Practice*, 22(7). Supersedes the original 2024 version (Furze, Perkins, Roe & MacVaugh, *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology*, 40(4), 38–55). Source PDFs in `context/JUTLP+Final+Perkins_JUTLP_2025-2.pdf` and `context/x3_9434_38-55.pdf`.

The AIAS gives instructors a **design tool**, not a security mechanism and not a value hierarchy — no level is "better" than another. The right level is a function of the learning outcome being assessed for a given task, not a campus-wide policy. (The 2025 revision deliberately dropped the original red→green traffic-light coloring because it implied a quality gradient that doesn't exist.)

## The 5 levels (2025 version — use these names/definitions by default)

| # | Name | Definition | Student-facing instruction (adapt wording, keep the substance) |
|---|------|------------|------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 1 | **No AI** | Completed entirely without AI assistance, in a controlled/invigilated environment. Students demonstrate core knowledge and skills solely from what they already know. | "You must not use AI at any point during this assessment. You must demonstrate your core skills and knowledge unaided." |
| 2 | **AI Planning** | AI may be used for pre-task brainstorming, outlining, and initial research. The focus is planning, synthesis, and ideation — not producing the final output. | "You may use AI for planning, idea development, and research. Your final submission should show how you developed and refined these ideas yourself." |
| 3 | **AI Collaboration** | AI helps complete the task itself (idea generation, drafting, feedback, refinement). Students must critically evaluate and modify anything AI produces before it counts as their work. | "You may use AI to assist with specific tasks such as drafting text or refining your work. You must critically evaluate and modify any AI-generated content you use." |
| 4 | **Full AI** | AI may complete any element of the task. The student's demonstrated skill shifts to directing AI effectively toward a goal and evaluating/critiquing the result. | "You may use AI extensively throughout this task. Focus on directing AI to achieve your goals while demonstrating your own critical thinking." |
| 5 | **AI Exploration** (new in 2025) | AI used creatively for problem-solving or novel insight, potentially co-designing the assessment approach itself with the instructor. | "You should use AI creatively to solve this task, potentially co-designing new approaches with your instructor." |

Note: the original 2024 paper used slightly different level 2–4 names (2 "AI-assisted idea generation and structuring," 3 "AI-assisted editing," 4 "AI task completion, human evaluation") and required an unedited-work appendix at level 3, which was dropped as unenforceable and inequitable. Default to the 2025 names above unless the instructor's institution has standardized on the 2024 wording.

## How to choose a level (design guidance, not just labeling)

1. **Start from the learning outcome**, not the assignment format. What is this specific task actually supposed to prove the student can do?
2. **Pick the AIAS level that fits that outcome**, then redesign the task mechanics around it — required evidence of process, staged checkpoints, a prompt log or reflection, revised marking criteria. *Relabeling an unchanged task "Level 3" without changing anything else is a discursive change, not a structural one — students notice and it accomplishes nothing.*
3. **Level 1 belongs in supervised/invigilated settings**, not honor-system take-home work — there's no way to actually assure AI-free conditions otherwise. If a program needs to preserve some fully-unaided assessment for validity, stage it at specific checkpoints (e.g., an oral defense, an in-class problem set) rather than declaring an entire take-home course AI-free.
4. **Levels 4–5 need deliberate redesign**, not just permission — e.g., requiring a rationale for AI-directed choices, or a reflection on what the student changed and why.
5. **A single course/syllabus rarely needs one blanket level.** Assign levels per component (see `templates/component-level-ai-policy-table.md`): e.g., Level 1 for self-reflections/peer feedback (only the student can reflect on their own experience), Level 2–4 for drafting or production tasks.
6. **Watch for equity effects at every level** — unequal access to paid AI tools and unequal prior AI literacy both distort what "demonstrating skill" means at levels 2–5. Flag this explicitly in policy language rather than assuming a level is neutral.
7. **Instructor policy should cover three areas, not just "what level":** ethics & transparency, security & privacy, and limitations & bias (e.g., hallucination risk, model bias) relevant to the discipline.

## Practical mapping instructors have used (from the AJET pilot implementation)

- Level 1: invigilated exams, professional-licensure-adjacent assessments only.
- Level 2: tasks that are specifically about language/writing skill in isolation.
- Level 3: treated as the "new base standard" for ungraded/low-stakes take-home work — least redesign needed from a pre-AI task.
- Level 4–5: reserved for higher-order tasks that have been deliberately redesigned around AI-direction and critical evaluation, not defaulted into.

That pilot (a single institution, reported as preliminary, not causal) saw AI-related misconduct cases drop from 77–87% of all misconduct (pre-AIAS) to under 17% (post-AIAS), alongside a reported ~33% rise in pass rates — cite as illustrative motivation, not proof, if summarizing evidence for an instructor.

## What this skill should NOT do

- Don't present the AIAS as a maturity ladder where higher = more advanced or more rigorous. Level 1 and Level 5 are equally legitimate for different outcomes.
- Don't assign one level to an entire syllabus by default — prompt the instructor to think per-component.
- Don't generate a bare numeric label without the structural task changes that make it real (evidence of process, checkpoints, or revised criteria appropriate to that level).
